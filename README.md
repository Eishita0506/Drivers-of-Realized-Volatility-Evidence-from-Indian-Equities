# Drivers of Realized Volatility Forecasting in an Emerging Market: Evidence from Indian Equities

# ABSTRACT
We examine the predictability, economic value, and risk-measurement performance of realized volatility forecasts for Indian equities using five-minute data on nine NSE indices spanning capitalization segments and five sectors over 2015–2026. India provides a distinctive setting for this question: the NSE’s 17-hour-45-minute non-trading interval encompasses the U.S. trading session, while overnight movements account for 24–44 percent of daily return variance and realized variance is highly skewed. We estimate thirty-five forecasting specifications per index and evaluate them out of sample using QLIKE, with formal inference based on Clark–West tests, false-discovery-rate control, and the Model Confidence Set.
The results reveal a clear hierarchy in what improves volatility forecasts. Modelling variance in logarithms rather than levels reduces out-of-sample loss by 25.7 percent, with level specifications excluded from the Model Confidence Set for seven of nine indices. Among the proposed extensions, heterogeneous leverage is the only systematic source of improvement, enhancing forecasts across all nine indices and surviving multiplicity control in six. Implied volatility adds value only when closely aligned with the index being forecast, while overnight returns and volatility of realized-volatility measures contribute little. By contrast, neither international information nor flexible machine-learning methods delivers a reliable out-of-sample gain: global variables are significant in-sample for four indices but improve forecasts in none, while eight machine-learning families fail to outperform the benchmark across all indices. A significant domestic control block in every index confirms that these null results reflect the information content of the data rather than the evaluation design.
Risk measurement yields a sharper conclusion. Conditional on the same variance forecast, the mapping from variance to the Value-at-Risk quantile matters substantially more than the choice among competing volatility models. Gaussian and Student-t plug-in mappings achieve correct coverage in only 73 of 169 model–index combinations and reach the Basel green zone in none, whereas a recursively calibrated mapping achieves correct coverage in 165 and reaches the green zone in 32. Thus, once volatility scale and asymmetry are adequately modelled, further expansion of the forecasting model offers limited gains; correctly specifying the conditional distribution is far more consequential for risk management. The evidence therefore favours parsimonious, economically structured volatility models and disciplined risk mapping over indiscriminate expansion of information sets or model flexibility.
